The 22 exam questions

From “Topics exam.pdf”

Clara's list, in her order. Each answer is what you should be able to write from memory: definition first, then an example. Tap a question to open the answer; tick it once you can say it without looking.

Q1What is marketing?

Understanding the customer so well that the product fits them and sells itself (Drucker): selling is not the main part. Kotler: marketing management is “the art and science of choosing target markets and building profitable relationships with them.”

More in What marketing is.

Q2What is market share?

A brand's sales as a % of the total market's sales, in value (€) or volume (units), usually from Nielsen sell-out data. Changes are reported in percentage points. Rising share means the brand is beating the market.

Example: mouthwash went from 23.9% to 27.1% of oral care. Brand metrics

Q3What is brand awareness?

The number or % of people who know the brand, measured at three levels: top of mind (named first), spontaneous (named unaided) and prompted (recognised when shown). The values people then link to it form the brand image.

Q4What is consumer experience?

How the customer perceives every interaction with the company and its product, website or app (UX), from first contact to after the purchase. UI is only how it looks on screen. Wii, iPad and Prius succeeded through good marketing homework plus an extraordinary UX.

Q5What framework do we use to analyse the macro situation?

PESTEL: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal. External forces the company can't control. PESTEL

Q6Value proposition, USP, competitive advantage?

Value proposition: a stated set of benefits that satisfies the customer's needs and why they should choose you over competitors (broad). USP: the one distinct feature that makes you stand out (narrow). Competitive advantage: the long-term edge that lets you outperform competitors.

Apple: easy, premium devices that work together / the camera / the ecosystem and brand loyalty. More

Q7What is the marketing mix?

The factors a company controls to influence purchase decisions: the 4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion). The 7Ps add People, Process and Physical evidence, for services and B2B. The 4Ps

Q8What are SMART goals?

Objectives that are Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant and Time-bound. “Increase email subscribers by 15% over the next three months through targeted social media campaigns.”

Q9What role do suppliers play in stakeholder analysis?

They help shape product development, inform you about new trends and materials, inform you about the competition, and influence your pricing strategy.

Q10In a SWOT, where do we talk about competitors?

In the Threats (external). Clara's slide: “Who are the competitors, and what threats do they pose?” IKEA: Wayfair, Amazon, local stores.

Q11What are the 4 criteria for B2C segmentation?

Demographic (who: age, gender, income), geographic (where: country, city, urban or rural), psychographic (why: lifestyle, values, personality), behavioural (how: usage, loyalty, benefits sought, online vs store). Segmentation

Q12Do B2C and B2B segmentation use the same criteria?

No. B2C segments individual consumers with the 4 criteria. B2B segments companies: industry, company size, location, revenue, technology used, how they buy (who decides, buying criteria), order size and urgency.

Not in Clara's slides; this is the standard Kotler answer.

Q13Which framework covers the stages a product goes through from introduction to decline?

The Product Life Cycle: (development) → introduction → growth → maturity → decline. Product life cycle

Q14Which tool identifies and assesses stakeholders?

Stakeholder analysis, mainly the Power/Interest Matrix (Mendelow's Matrix), which plots stakeholders by power and interest to prioritise them: key players, keep satisfied, keep informed, low priority. Mendelow's matrix

Q15What is relationship marketing?

Building rich, multifaceted, long-term relationships with customers, channel members and other marketing partners. One of the 4 parts of holistic marketing. The aim is loyalty and customer lifetime value, not one-off sales.

Q16What is a “niche” targeting strategy?

Concentrating on one small, well-defined segment with specific needs that bigger competitors ignore, and serving it better than anyone. Also called concentrated marketing. Example: Ferrari.

Not in Clara's slides; standard textbook answer. Targeting

Q17What is the primary goal of market segmentation?

To divide a broad market into smaller groups with shared characteristics, so you can find the segments with the greatest opportunity and tailor your offer to them more efficiently, because not everyone wants the same thing.

Q18What is the latest trend in market segmentation?

Using AI to personalise and reach very specific segments (Clara's exact slide). Products move away from standardisation towards niche and customisation.

Q19Why do we do stakeholder analysis?

To understand each stakeholder's expectations, power and likely reaction, so we can prioritise and manage them. Benefits: better communication, risk management, informed decisions, conflict prevention.

Q20What do we do in competition analysis?

Identify and evaluate competitors' strengths, weaknesses, strategies and performance (product, pricing, distribution, branding, customer service, market share, financials) to understand the market, spot opportunities and threats, and position ourselves better.

Q21Who are the “early adopters” in the innovation curve?

The second group to buy (13.5%), after the innovators. The most influential opinion leaders: active on social media, writing reviews, with fairly high status, education and income. Less risky than innovators; they look for more information first. Winning them is the key to crossing the chasm to the mass market. Innovation curve

Q22What is benchmarking?

Comparing your performance, processes or offerings with the best in the industry (or direct competitors) to find gaps, areas to improve and best practices. Three types: competitive, functional, internal. Benchmarking

›What marketing isDrucker, Kotler, what marketing management does
Drucker: the aim of marketing is to know and understand the customer so well that the product fits them and sells itself. Selling is not the most important part. Q1
Kotler (“father of modern marketing”): marketing management is “the art and science of choosing target markets and building profitable relationships with them.” A process of analysis, planning, implementing and control, covering goods, services and ideas.

UX vs UI. UX = how a user perceives an interaction with a company's website or product. UI = how it looks on the screen. Wii, iPad and Prius were swamped with orders because they were built on careful marketing homework about consumers, competition and external factors, and offered an extraordinary UX.

What the marketing department decides

Whom the company wants as customers · which needs to satisfy · what products to offer · what prices to set · what communications to send and receive · which distribution channels to use · which partnerships to develop. Marketing only succeeds when all departments work together (engineering, finance, purchasing, production, accounting).

The 6 functions of marketing management

  1. Assessing marketing opportunities
  2. Planning marketing activities
  3. Organising marketing activities
  4. Coordinating the company's different activities
  5. Directing and motivating employees
  6. Evaluating and controlling marketing efforts (compare actual vs planned, fix the gaps)

The 8 tasks in marketing management

Develop strategies and plans · capture marketing insights · connect with customers · build strong brands · create value · deliver value · communicate value · conduct marketing responsibly for long-term success.

›Needs, wants, demandMaslow, 5 types of needs, 8 demand states
NeedA basic human requirement: food, water, shelter, recreation, education.
WantA need aimed at a specific object: a deep-dish pizza, a Mercedes. Shaped by society.
DemandA want for a specific product backed by the ability to pay.

Marketers don't create needs: needs exist before marketers. An ad can say a Mercedes satisfies your need for status; it didn't create that need.

Maslow's pyramid of needs (bottom to top)

  1. Physiological: air, water, food, shelter, sleep, clothing
  2. Safety: personal security, employment, resources, health, property
  3. Love and belonging: friendship, intimacy, family, connection
  4. Esteem: respect, self-esteem, status, recognition
  5. Self-actualisation: becoming the most one can be

The 5 types of needs (car buyer)

NeedCar buyer example
Stated“I want an inexpensive car.”
RealA car whose operating cost, not initial price, is low.
UnstatedExpects good service from the dealer, never says it.

Responding only to the stated need can shortchange the customer. Companies must help customers learn what they want (Apple shaping how people saw tablets).

›Brand metricsAwareness, image, penetration, market share
Brand awareness = number or % of people who know the brand. Q3
Top of mindThe first brand named, unaided.
SpontaneousAny brand named without help.
PromptedRecognised when shown or read the name.

Always: top of mind ≤ spontaneous ≤ prompted. After awareness, the values associated with the brand define its brand image.

Market share = your sales as a % of the total market's sales, in value (€) or volume (units). Data comes from sell-out panels bought from suppliers like Nielsen (also Kantar, GfK, Gartner, IDG). YTD = year to date. Q2

Listerine example: total oral care market 421,820 thousand € (+10%). Mouthwash's share of oral care: 23.9% (Jul 02) → 25.7% → 25.9% → 27.1% (Apr 05), reported as +1.8 p, +0.2 p, +1.2 p (percentage points).

Penetration = % of people who use the product or category. Know / use / sell awareness / penetration / share.

Building strong brands

  1. Awareness: people must know the brand exists
  2. Trust: brand reputation = trust
  3. Trial: they try it (promotion)
›ValueValue proposition, offering, brand, value triad, satisfaction
Value proposition = a stated set of benefits that satisfies the customer's needs, and why they should pick you over competitors. Q6

Offering = the value proposition made real: a combination of products, services, information and experiences. Brand = an offering from a known source. Apple's associations: creative, innovative, easy-to-use, fun, cool.

Customer value triad = quality + service + price. Perceived value rises with quality and service, falls with price.

DisappointedPerformance below expectations
SatisfiedPerformance matches expectations
DelightedPerformance exceeds expectations
›Holistic marketing, new 4Ps, 4AsRelationship marketing lives here

Holistic marketing = designing marketing programmes that recognise how broad and interconnected today's marketing is. Four components:

ComponentMeaning
InternalEveryone in the organisation, especially senior management, embraces marketing principles.
IntegratedAll ways of creating, delivering and communicating value are combined in the best way.
Relationship Q15Rich, multifaceted relationships with customers, channel members and other marketing partners.
PerformanceUnderstanding the returns of marketing (financial and non-financial: brand and customer equity) plus legal, ethical, social and environmental effects.

The new 4Ps (holistic version)

  • People: employees are critical to marketing success (internal marketing); see consumers as people, not just shoppers.
  • Processes: the creativity, discipline and structure of marketing management.
  • Programs: all consumer-directed activities, including the old 4Ps, integrated so the whole is greater than the sum.
  • Performance: financial and non-financial outcomes, and effects beyond the company.

›Media and channelsPaid/owned/earned, native, impressions, the 3 channels
PPaidYou pay a fee: TV, display ads, paid search, sponsorships, paid influencers, retargeting.
OOwnedChannels you own: website, mobile site, blog, brand social accounts.
EEarnedOthers talk about you voluntarily: mentions, shares, reposts, reviews, word of mouth.

“Digital marketing trifecta”: paid promotion propels sharing (earned), SEO and owned content drive earned traffic, paid gives owned properties more exposure.

Native advertising = paid ads made to blend in with the platform's own content (“sponsored”, “branded”), which reduces ad recognition. Still paid.

Impressions = people saw it (reach), no insight into results. Engagement = active involvement (likes, comments, shares); more valuable. Three “screens” reach consumers: TV, internet, mobile; 3 in 5 consumers use two screens at once.

The 3 marketing channels

ChannelJob
CommunicationDeliver and receive messages: media, stores, websites; dialogue (email, blogs) and monologue (ads).
DistributionDisplay, sell or deliver the product. Direct (internet, phone) or indirect (wholesalers, retailers, agents).
ServiceSupport the transaction: warehouses, transport, banks, insurers.

Media history and today's numbers

  • 19th century: Gutenberg's printing press → daily newspapers. 1836: La Presse is the first paper to use paid advertising to lower its price.
  • 20th century: radio (1920s consumerism), TV boom after WWII, cable in the 80s–90s. In the 90s a prime-time TV slot reached up to 20 million viewers.
  • 2020: print readership −34%, TV viewers −10%, out-of-home and cinema stable, online +140% in a decade.
  • The average person spends 2½ hours a day on social media (one day a week online). Over half the world owns a smartphone.
  • You no longer buy a specific medium; you buy a specific audience across many platforms.
›The digital shiftCustomer-centric marketing, new capabilities
  • From product-centric to customer-centric: from managing a product portfolio to managing a customer portfolio. Databases let you understand customers better and send personalised offers. Less standardisation, more niche and customisation.
  • From monologues to conversations: company messages are a small fraction of the online conversation about a product. The power of customers grows.
  • Digital metrics: consumer profitability, customer lifetime value (CLV), return on marketing investment (ROMI). Detailed, accurate analysis of sales and competition; adaptable strategy.
  • Ethics: marketing decisions have consequences.
New consumer capabilitiesSearch and buy on the move, share opinions and loyalty on social media, interact with companies, reject marketing they dislike.
New company capabilitiesInternet as an information and sales channel, richer data on markets and competitors, fast targeted reach via social and mobile, better internal processes, cost efficiency.
›Marketing environment and supply chainTask vs broad environment, value delivery

Core marketing concepts (Clara's list): needs, wants and demands · target markets, positioning, segmentation · offerings and brands · marketing channels · paid, owned, earned media · impressions and engagement · value and satisfaction · supply chain · competition · marketing environment.

Task environmentThe players in producing, distributing and promoting the offering: suppliers (materials and services like research or ad agencies, banks, transport), distributors and dealers, target customers.
Broad environmentSix components: demographic, economic, social-cultural, natural, technological, political-legal. Track trends and adjust strategy.

Supply chain = the channel from raw materials to components to finished products carried to final buyers. Each company captures only part of the total value; acquiring competitors or expanding upstream or downstream captures more. Coffee example: Ethiopian farmers → Fair Trade cooperative → Alternative Trading Organisation (pays at least $1.26 a pound) → developed-world retail.

›Market size and competitionTAM, SAM, SOM; direct vs indirect

Competition = all actual and potential rival offerings and substitutes. Direct: same product (Netflix vs Disney+). Indirect: different product, same need (Netflix vs cinema).

›Market researchQuant vs qual, methods, facts vs insights

Analyse marketing opportunities by looking at four things: the market, the suppliers, the competition, the consumer. Consumer analysis asks: who are your customers, what do they need, what drives their decision, how does your product meet their needs.

QuantitativeBig samples (ideally ~1,000). The what and how many. Gives facts (%), from closed questions.
QualitativeSmall groups, open questions. The why. Gives insights (feelings).
MethodTypeDetails
In-depth interviewsQual1h–1h30, 10–20 people. Heavy users, key opinion leaders, non-users; find insights for communication.
Focus groupsQual6–8 people per group, 4–8 groups, 1h30–2h. Rich discussion; test how messages land.
U&A (usage & attitude)QuantLong questionnaire, a snapshot in time; compares countries, defines clusters. Penetration, reasons to buy or not, moment and frequency of use.
Historical trackingQuantSame metrics, method and supplier for a long time: penetration, awareness, reasons for not using, recommendation.
OmnibusQuantOne survey shared by several clients, who split the cost. Cheaper; your own study is pricier but more flexible.

Fact: “27% of the population use mouthwash.” Insight: “I feel confident once I know 99% of my germs are killed.” Market insight = discovering a relevant, actionable, previously unrealised reality about a target market. Research output feeds the value proposition.

B2C customer research sample: profile, income, age range, lifestyle, location, scope (number of potential customers), customer base (share), purchase history.

Online tools: Google Forms, SurveyMonkey. Social media research: polls, contests, call-to-action posts, brand mentions, Instagram question stickers and emoji sliders.

Common mistakes stopping a historical tracking, changing the sample or the question, the wrong target (a cynical person can wreck a focus group's dynamic). Ask users and buyers.

›Marketing and society9 roles, social responsibility

Marketing's 9 roles in society: facilitating economic growth · satisfying consumer needs · encouraging innovation · supporting social change (anti-smoking, recycling) · creating jobs · providing information · cultural influence and identity · improving standards of living · enhancing brand and community engagement (CSR).

Social responsibility (CSR): the company doing things for society. Customers engage with brands with a good image; employees who share the company's values are more productive and motivated. Gen Z (born ~1997–2012) was predicted to be ~27% of the workforce by 2025, and climate is a primary concern for them.

›Case studiesWhat they are, types, why companies use them

Key elements: introduction (company, background) → the problem the client needed to solve → the solution (and why your company was the right fit) → the result (data before and after).

Third-person / clientOne client's experience with your company or product.
ExplanatoryThe impact of a marketing strategy on growth; observation and inference, not first-hand.
ImplementationGoes further, covering the actual implementation in detail.

Why use them: in 2020 only 14% of consumers trusted advertising to be honest; 91% of 18–34-year-olds trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations; a customer testimonial can raise conversion by up to 34%. Companies need social proof. Video case studies are growing (L'Oréal × YouTube: No. 2 in category, 34% of mass online sales).

Case examples Clara listed: McDonald's (SWOT 2018), Spotify, Netflix, IKEA, Zara, Starbucks, Patagonia, Apple, Nike, Lush, LEGO, Dove.

›PESTELThe macro framework, with every example from the slides

Trap Classify by the cause, not by who is affected. Inflation making students cancel apps = economic, not social. A tax change = political; a data-privacy law = legal.

›Porter's 5 ForcesHow intense competition is, so how profitable the industry is

By Michael E. Porter. Every force is someone who can take your profit; the stronger the forces, the harder it is to make money.

ForceQuestion + driversApple / smartphones
Threat of new entrantsHow easily can new firms get in? Barriers: capital, brand loyalty, economies of scale, regulation, access to distribution, switching costsModerate–high (Xiaomi, OnePlus)
Supplier powerCan suppliers push prices up? High when suppliers are few, specialised, unique, or costly to switchModerate (Qualcomm chips, Samsung OLEDs)
Buyer powerCan customers push prices down? High with many alternatives, price sensitivity, big orders, good informationHigh
Threat of substitutesCan customers switch to a different product meeting the same need? Caps price increasesHigh (tablets, feature phones)
RivalryHow hard do existing players fight? Number of competitors, industry growth, exit barriers, price and innovation warsVery high (Samsung, Google, Huawei)

Trap Low barriers to entry = high threat of new entrants. Mercadona buying yoghurt = buyer power for the yoghurt brand. YouTube videos vs a fitness app = substitute; another workout app = rival.

›Market share in a changing marketClara's class exercise
  • Can share rise while the market falls? Yes: market −20%, your sales −10% → your slice grows.
  • Can share fall while the market grows? Yes: market +30%, you +15% → rivals grew faster.
  • Rising share shows the brand performs above the market, whether the market grows or shrinks.
